      Maui’s North Shore has always faced the open ocean

      The ocean was a road, pantry, classroom and source of genealogy for Native Hawaiians. Skilled Polynesian voyagers reached Hawaiʻi by reading stars, currents, birds, clouds and swells across thousands of miles.

      Today’s connection: At Ho‘okipa and Kū‘au Bay, the surf is not just scenery—it is part of a living seafaring culture. Watch the water with a little extra wonder before Mama’s.

      Ka‘anapali once belonged to a much older landscape

      Before resorts, Maui was organized into moku and ahupuaʻa—land divisions that often ran from mountain to sea so communities could access water, forest, farmland and fishing grounds.

      Plantation history created today’s local food culture

      Sugar plantations drew workers from Japan, China, Portugal, the Philippines, Korea, Puerto Rico and elsewhere. Their foods and traditions mingled with Native Hawaiian life, helping create Hawaiʻi’s distinctive local culture.

      A few beautiful words

      A tiny Hawaiian language shelf

      Words carry worldview. Pronunciation and meaning can vary by context, so consider these invitations to learn—not a complete language lesson.

      AlohaLove, compassion, peace and the presence shared between people—not merely “hello” and “goodbye.”
      MālamaTo care for, preserve and protect. You will often hear mālama ʻāina: care for the land.
      MoʻoleloStory, history, tradition or narrative—the kind of memory this website will become for our family.
      Mauka / MakaiToward the mountain / toward the sea—directions grounded in the landscape rather than north and south.
      KuleanaA responsibility, privilege and area of care. Visiting thoughtfully is part of a traveler’s kuleana.
      MahaloGratitude, admiration or thanks—a lovely word, but even lovelier as a practice.
